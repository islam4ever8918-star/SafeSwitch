The transition to the post-quantum era

The future of computing will change the rules of digital trust.

Quantum computers introduce a fundamentally different computational model. Some of the mathematical assumptions protecting today's Internet could eventually become vulnerable.

Post-quantum cryptography is the engineering effort to prepare for that world before it arrives.

01 / The big picture

Why quantum computing matters to cybersecurity.

Modern digital security is built on mathematical assumptions. We use those assumptions because certain mathematical problems are extremely difficult for classical computers to solve.

Quantum computing introduces algorithms that can dramatically change the difficulty of some of those problems.

01

Classical computers

Traditional computers operate using bits. A bit represents one of two logical states: zero or one. Modern processors can manipulate billions of bits extremely quickly, but their underlying computational model remains classical.

02

Quantum computers

Quantum computers use qubits and exploit phenomena such as superposition, entanglement and interference. They are designed to solve particular classes of problems using quantum mechanics.

03

Cryptography

Cryptography protects communication, authentication, software updates, digital identities, financial transactions and infrastructure. Its security depends on mathematical assumptions.

02 / Quantum computing

A quantum computer is not simply a faster computer.

The important difference is the computational model. Quantum computers manipulate quantum states. Their power comes from carefully controlling quantum amplitudes and interference, not simply from having a faster CPU.

Classical bit

A classical bit has one logical value at a time.

bit ∈ { 0, 1 }

Classical computing represents information using combinations of these binary states.

Qubit

A qubit can be described using a superposition of computational basis states.

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩

The complex amplitudes satisfy:

|α|² + |β|² = 1
CONCEPT 01

Superposition

Quantum states can exist as combinations of basis states. The amplitudes associated with those states can later interfere with one another.

CONCEPT 02

Entanglement

Multiple quantum systems can become correlated in ways that cannot be described as independent classical states.

CONCEPT 03

Interference

Quantum amplitudes can reinforce or cancel one another. Quantum algorithms exploit this behavior to increase useful outcomes and suppress others.

03 / Scaling

The state space grows exponentially.

With n qubits, the mathematical state is described over 2ⁿ computational basis states. That does not mean we can simply read 2ⁿ classical answers from a measurement. Quantum algorithms must engineer interference so that useful information can actually be extracted.

A critical clarification

Saying that a quantum computer has 2ⁿ possible basis states does not mean it can calculate 2ⁿ independent classical answers and return them all. Quantum algorithms are powerful because they manipulate amplitudes and interference in ways that can provide speedups for specific problems.

04 / The cryptographic threat

Quantum computing does not break everything. It changes specific mathematical assumptions.

The most important cryptographic quantum threat comes from algorithms such as Shor's algorithm. A different class of impact comes from Grover's algorithm.

QUANTUM ALGORITHM

Shor's algorithm

Shor's algorithm provides an efficient quantum approach to integer factorization and discrete logarithms. Those mathematical problems are closely connected to the security foundations of widely used public-key systems.

This is why RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography are central concerns in the transition to post-quantum cryptography.

QUANTUM ALGORITHM

Grover's algorithm

Grover's algorithm provides a quadratic speedup for unstructured search. Its impact on symmetric cryptography is therefore fundamentally different from Shor's impact on public-key cryptography.

The practical response includes selecting appropriate symmetric key sizes and security margins.

05 / Shor's algorithm

Why public-key cryptography is the main concern.

Public-key cryptography allows strangers to communicate securely and authenticate one another without first sharing a secret key. The security of many classical systems depends on mathematical problems believed to be difficult.

RSA

Factoring

RSA uses arithmetic involving large integers. The security of properly configured RSA depends on the practical difficulty of factoring large numbers using known classical methods.

A sufficiently powerful fault-tolerant quantum computer running Shor's algorithm would change that assumption.

ECC

Discrete logarithms

Elliptic-curve cryptography uses the mathematical structure of elliptic curves. Protocols and signatures based on elliptic-curve discrete logarithms are also threatened by quantum algorithms.

06 / Harvest now, decrypt later

The quantum problem starts before quantum computers arrive.

Encrypted information can have a lifetime much longer than the systems that originally transmitted it. An attacker can capture encrypted traffic today and retain it for future cryptanalysis.

07 / Post-quantum cryptography

PQC is the cryptographic answer to the quantum threat.

Post-quantum cryptography describes cryptographic algorithms designed to remain secure against known classical and quantum attack techniques. They run on conventional computers.

PQ

FAMILY

Lattice-based

Lattice cryptography uses hard mathematical problems involving high-dimensional lattices. Modern standardized PQC algorithms such as ML-KEM and ML-DSA are based on structured lattice problems.

FAMILY

Hash-based

Hash-based signatures build digital signatures from cryptographic hash functions. They provide an important alternative construction with different operational properties.

FAMILY

Code-based

Code-based cryptography derives security from difficult decoding problems associated with error-correcting codes.

08 / Key establishment

ML-KEM: building a shared secret in a post-quantum world.

ML-KEM is a standardized post-quantum key encapsulation mechanism. It is designed to allow communicating parties to establish shared secret material without relying on classical public-key assumptions vulnerable to quantum attacks.

What ML-KEM does

ML-KEM is used for key establishment rather than directly encrypting large application data streams.

Key establishment → shared secret → symmetric encryption

Once both parties possess shared secret material, efficient symmetric cryptography can protect the actual application traffic.

CORE IDEA

Keep bulk encryption symmetric.

Public-key and KEM operations are comparatively expensive. Symmetric authenticated encryption is designed for efficiently protecting large volumes of traffic.

This division of responsibility is fundamental to practical secure networking.

09 / Digital signatures

ML-DSA: protecting identity and integrity.

Confidentiality is only one part of security. Infrastructure must also know who is allowed to issue commands, publish configuration, deploy software and identify itself as a trusted node.

Node identity

Infrastructure nodes need cryptographic identities so that other components can distinguish legitimate systems from unauthorized systems.

Configuration

Critical routing and failover policies can be authenticated and digitally signed.

Software updates

Digital signatures can help verify that software packages and updates came from an authorized source and were not modified.

Encryption and signatures solve different problems.

A KEM such as ML-KEM is associated with establishing shared secret material. A signature algorithm such as ML-DSA is associated with authenticity and integrity. A complete PQC architecture should consider both.

10 / Hybrid cryptography

The transition does not have to be all-or-nothing.

During migration, organizations can use classical and post-quantum mechanisms together where protocols and implementations support appropriate hybrid constructions.

01

Classical key exchange

Preserve established security mechanisms during transition.

02

03

Combine

04

Session key

Establish efficient symmetric session encryption.

05

Protect application data using symmetric authenticated encryption.

11 / TLS 1.3

You do not need to invent a new Internet protocol.

The goal of PQC migration is not to throw away decades of protocol engineering. Mature protocols such as TLS provide an established framework into which new cryptographic mechanisms can be integrated.

TLS remains the framework.

TLS provides authenticated secure communication. PQC can change the underlying cryptographic mechanisms used for key establishment and authentication while retaining the overall protocol architecture.

PQC changes the cryptographic components.

Migration can affect handshake sizes, certificates, computational cost, key establishment and signatures. These effects need to be measured rather than assumed.

14 / DDoS

PQC and XDP solve different security problems.

One protects mathematical trust assumptions. The other allows infrastructure to process and discard packets extremely early. Combining both can produce a stronger architecture.

PROBLEM

Volumetric attacks

Attackers attempt to overwhelm bandwidth, network interfaces, connection capacity or application resources.

DEFENSE

XDP

XDP can discard unwanted traffic at an extremely early point in Linux networking, reducing the amount of work performed by later layers.

TRUST

PQC

PQC protects the cryptographic mechanisms used for identity, key establishment and signatures.

A necessary limitation

Local packet filtering cannot recover bandwidth that has already been exhausted upstream. If an attack saturates the network path before traffic reaches infrastructure controlled by SafeSwitch, upstream routing, capacity, filtering or DDoS-scrubbing mechanisms are required.

16 / Migration

Becoming post-quantum is a systems-engineering project.

The hardest part is often not selecting an algorithm. It is discovering where cryptography exists throughout a large organization.

PHASE 01

Inventory

Identify certificates, keys, protocols, libraries, applications, VPNs, APIs, embedded devices, identity systems and long-lived encrypted data.

PHASE 02

Classify

Determine which systems depend on RSA, ECC or other quantum-vulnerable mechanisms.

PHASE 03

Test

Measure CPU cost, memory requirements, handshake sizes, bandwidth overhead and interoperability.

PHASE 04

Hybrid deployment

Where appropriate, introduce post-quantum mechanisms alongside established classical mechanisms.

PHASE 05

Migration

Gradually remove vulnerable dependencies while preserving operational continuity.

PHASE 06

Continuous monitoring

Cryptographic recommendations evolve. Security architecture therefore needs to remain adaptable.

17 / Engineering reality

Post-quantum security has real engineering costs.

PQC should not be treated as a magic checkbox. New algorithms can change bandwidth requirements, handshake sizes, CPU usage, memory requirements and implementation complexity.

18 / What PQC does not solve

Quantum resistance is not the same thing as total security.

It does not stop DDoS attacks.

PQC protects cryptographic assumptions. It does not increase network capacity or automatically absorb volumetric attacks.

It does not fix compromised endpoints.

If an authorized endpoint is compromised, encryption alone cannot make that endpoint trustworthy again.

It does not automatically secure BGP.

Routing security is a separate engineering domain. BGP security mechanisms and operational controls remain important.

It does not eliminate every attack.

Authentication, authorization, isolation, secure software development, monitoring, key management and incident response remain necessary.

19 / The complete model

Resilient infrastructure for a post-quantum Internet.

The most powerful architecture is layered. Network routing handles global reachability. XDP handles early packet processing. eBPF provides kernel-level intelligence. Failover provides resilience. PQC protects long-term cryptographic trust.

20 / Frequently asked questions

The questions that matter.

Is a quantum computer going to break the Internet tomorrow?
No. Cryptographically relevant quantum computing requires substantial advances in quantum hardware, error correction and fault-tolerant computation. The reason migration is being discussed now is that cryptographic infrastructure can take years to replace, while sensitive information may need protection for much longer.
Does post-quantum cryptography require a quantum computer?
No. PQC algorithms are intended to run on conventional computing infrastructure.
Does PQC replace TLS?
Not necessarily. PQC provides cryptographic mechanisms. Protocols such as TLS can incorporate suitable post-quantum mechanisms into their key-establishment and authentication systems.
Is AES broken by quantum computing?
AES is not threatened in the same way RSA and ECC are. Grover's algorithm gives a quadratic speedup for certain search problems, so selecting appropriate symmetric key sizes remains important.
Why not simply wait for quantum computers?
Large organizations have enormous cryptographic dependencies. Certificates, embedded systems, legacy software, network equipment and long-lived sensitive information cannot necessarily be migrated overnight.
Should PQC operations run inside eBPF?
Heavy public-key cryptographic operations generally should not be inserted into the per-packet hot path. For an architecture such as SafeSwitch, PQC is more naturally applied to the control plane, node identity, configuration signing, software updates and secure management communication.
Does PQC make SafeSwitch impossible to attack?
No. PQC strengthens cryptographic assumptions against quantum attacks. It does not eliminate software bugs, compromised endpoints, routing attacks, DDoS attacks, configuration errors or operational failures.
What is the biggest idea behind all of this?
Cryptographic agility. Infrastructure should be designed so that cryptographic mechanisms can evolve without requiring the entire system to be rebuilt.
THE POST-QUANTUM ERA

Build systems that can survive both infrastructure failure and changing mathematics.

Quantum computing changes some of the mathematical assumptions that modern cryptography depends on. Post-quantum cryptography is the beginning of the response. But the bigger lesson is architectural: build infrastructure that can evolve.

Keep the data plane fast. Keep failure detection independent. Keep trust cryptographically strong. Keep algorithms replaceable. Keep the system distributed. And assume that today's best security architecture will eventually need to evolve again.

Fast data plane + Global resilience + Independent monitoring + Post-quantum trust

= Infrastructure designed for the next Internet.
